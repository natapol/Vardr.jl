Case study: a bioprocess twin

This chapter builds a twin of a fed-batch bioreactor that makes a recombinant protein, the workhorse process of biopharmaceutical manufacturing. It follows the Step-by-step workflow on a realistic industrial problem, explains the bioprocess engineering behind each modelling choice, and ends with a decision that the twin gets right and an uninformed model gets wrong.

You will see how to:

  • build a model from mass balances and classical kinetics, with a feed profile driven by parameters,
  • combine fast online sensors with slow, sparse laboratory assays (NaN entries),
  • use a nonlinear observation function that depends on estimated parameters (the oxygen uptake rate),
  • learn four batch-specific unknowns at once, including a hidden equipment fault,
  • turn the twin's forecasts into a risk-constrained decision.

The same code is available as a script: examples/bioreactor_fedbatch_twin.jl. For a twin of a patient rather than a process, see examples/pkpd_patient_twin.jl (see Examples).

All code on this page runs when the documentation is built.

using Vardr, Distributions, LinearAlgebra, Random, Statistics, DataFrames

Why twins in pharmaceutical manufacturing?

Biologics are made by living cells, and no two batches behave the same: the inoculum, raw-material lots and equipment all vary. Quality has traditionally been ensured by running a fixed recipe and testing the product afterwards. Regulators now encourage the opposite approach. Under Process Analytical Technology (PAT), quality is built in by measuring and controlling the process while it runs (U.S. Food and Drug Administration, 2004). Under Quality by Design (QbD), the manufacturer understands how process parameters affect quality, and operates within a justified design space (International Council for Harmonisation, 2009).

Both need what a digital twin provides: estimates of quantities that cannot be measured online (product concentration, specific rates, yields), predictions of where the batch is heading, and the ability to test an action before taking it. Mechanistic process models are the natural basis (Narayanan et al., 2020). Combined with state estimation they are known in bioprocessing as soft sensors (Luttmann et al., 2012; Dochain, 2003).

1. The process and the decision

Escherichia coli expressing a recombinant protein is grown in a 5 L bioreactor:

  1. Batch phase (0 to about 7 h). The medium starts with 20 g/L glucose. Cells grow at their maximum rate until the glucose runs out.
  2. Fed-batch phase (7 h to harvest at 36 h). Concentrated glucose (500 g/L) is pumped in. The feed rate rises exponentially so that growth is held at a chosen, sub-maximal rate. This is the standard way to reach high cell density while avoiding overflow metabolism: when E. coli takes up glucose faster than it can respire it, the excess is excreted as acetate, which inhibits growth and protein production (Lee, 1996; Yee and Blanch, 1992; Eiteman and Altman, 2006).

The feed cannot rise forever. Cells need oxygen to burn glucose, and their oxygen uptake rate (OUR) grows with the feed. A vessel can transfer only a limited amount of oxygen from the gas to the liquid. The transfer rate is $\mathrm{OTR} = k_La\,(C^* - C_L)$, where $k_La$ is the volumetric mass-transfer coefficient and $C^* - C_L$ the dissolved-oxygen driving force (Garcia-Ochoa and Gomez, 2009). With agitation and aeration at their maximum, this vessel can supply $\mathrm{OTR}_{\max} = 180$ mmol O₂/L/h. If the demand exceeds it, dissolved oxygen collapses, metabolism turns to acetate, and the batch may be lost.

So the feed must be capped. At t = 20 h the process engineer must choose the cap $F_\text{cap}$ for the rest of the batch:

Choose the largest feed cap such that the probability of the oxygen demand exceeding $\mathrm{OTR}_{\max}$ before harvest is at most 5%.

A higher cap means more biomass and more product, but more oxygen risk. The right cap depends on things nobody knows exactly for this batch: how fast these cells grow, how efficiently they turn glucose into biomass, and how much glucose the pump really delivers. This is the twin's job.

2. The process model

Mass balances. For a well-mixed reactor fed at rate $F$ (L/h) with no outflow, the volume grows as $\mathrm dV/\mathrm dt = F$. For any dissolved or suspended component with concentration $c$ and net production rate $r$, the amount $cV$ obeys $\mathrm d(cV)/\mathrm dt = rV + F c_\text{feed}$. Expanding gives a production term plus a dilution term $-(F/V)\,c$:

\[\begin{aligned} \frac{\mathrm dX}{\mathrm dt} &= \mu X - \frac{F}{V}X &&\text{biomass (g/L)}\\ \frac{\mathrm dS}{\mathrm dt} &= -q_S X + \frac{F}{V}(S_f - S) &&\text{glucose (g/L)}\\ \frac{\mathrm dP}{\mathrm dt} &= q_P X - \frac{F}{V}P &&\text{product (g/L)}\\ \frac{\mathrm dV}{\mathrm dt} &= F &&\text{volume (L)} \end{aligned}\]

Kinetics. The specific rates use three classical laws:

  • Growth follows Monod kinetics (Monod, 1949): $\mu = \mu_{\max} S/(K_s + S)$. It is nearly $\mu_{\max}$ when glucose is plentiful and proportional to $S$ when it is scarce.
  • Glucose uptake pays for growth and for cell maintenance (Pirt, 1965): $q_S = \mu/Y_{X/S} + m_S\,S/(K_s+S)$. $Y_{X/S}$ is the biomass yield (g of cells per g of glucose). The factor $S/(K_s+S)$ switches maintenance off when there is no glucose left to consume.
  • Product formation follows the Luedeking–Piret law (Luedeking and Piret, 1959): $q_P = \alpha\mu + q_p$. It has a growth-associated part and a non-growth-associated part, the specific productivity $q_p$.

Feed. To hold growth at a set rate $\mu_\text{set}$ under glucose limitation, the glucose fed must equal the glucose consumed: $F S_f = q_S X V$ with $\mu = \mu_\text{set}$. Because $XV$ then grows like $e^{\mu_\text{set} t}$, so must the feed (Lee, 1996):

\[F(t) = f_\text{pump}\,\min\!\Big(F_0\, e^{\mu_\text{set}(t - t_\text{feed})},\ F_\text{cap}\Big), \qquad F_0 = \frac{(\mu_\text{set}/Y_{X/S} + m_S)\,X V}{S_f}\bigg|_{t_\text{feed}} .\]

$F_0$ is designed from the process development (PD) values: $X \approx 10$ g/L, $V = 5$ L and $\mu_\text{set} = 0.1$ h⁻¹. The factor $f_\text{pump}$ is the ratio of delivered to requested flow. It should be 1, but pumps drift, and tubing wears and creeps. In the model it is just another parameter, which the twin can estimate.

const PNAMES = [:mu_max, :Ks, :Yxs, :ms, :alpha, :qp, :kO, :Sf,
    :F0, :mu_set, :t_feed, :F_cap, :f_pump]

feedrate(p, t) = t < p[11] ? 0.0 : p[13] * min(p[9] * exp(p[10] * (t - p[11])), p[12])
uptake(S, p) = (p[1] / p[3] + p[4]) * S / (p[2] + S)   # specific glucose uptake q_S (g/g/h)

function bioreactor(u, p, t)
    X, S, P, V = u
    Sp = max(S, 0.0)
    mu = p[1] * Sp / (p[2] + Sp)
    F = feedrate(p, t)
    D = F / V                                          # dilution rate (1/h)
    return [mu * X - D * X,
            -uptake(Sp, p) * X + D * (p[8] - S),
            (p[5] * mu + p[6]) * X - D * P,
            F]
end

# Process development values (the "recipe"); F_cap = Inf means no cap yet
pd = [0.6, 0.05, 0.5, 0.04, 0.01, 0.004, 12.0, 500.0,
    0.024, 0.1, 7.0, Inf, 1.0]
model = TwinModel(bioreactor, [0.2, 20.0, 0.0, 5.0], pd;
    states = [:X, :S, :P, :V], params = PNAMES, tstops = [7.0])
TwinModel(4 states [:X, :S, :P, :V], 13 parameters, Tsit5)

tstops = [7.0] makes the solver stop exactly when feeding starts, because the right-hand side jumps there (see Models and sensors). All feed settings are parameters, so a what-if scenario can change them with SetParameter.

3. The sensors

A modern bioreactor has several PAT measurements, at very different rates:

At-line glucose analyserglucose $S$4 h0.1 g/L
Offline HPLCproduct titer $P$4 h0.05 g/L
  • Capacitance probes (dielectric spectroscopy) respond to cells with intact membranes, so they measure viable biomass online (Carvell and Dowd, 2006).
  • The off-gas analyser compares the O₂ fraction in the inlet and exhaust gas. With the gas flow, this gives the OUR. OUR is not a state: it is a function of the state and of the parameters. Each gram of glucose taken up consumes $k_O$ mmol O₂, a stoichiometric coefficient from elemental balances (here 12 mmol/g, assumed known):

    \[\mathrm{OUR} = k_O\, q_S(S;\ \mu_{\max}, Y_{X/S}, m_S)\, X .\]

    This is a nonlinear observation operator that depends on estimated parameters. The twin evaluates it with each ensemble member's own $\mu_{\max}$ and $Y_{X/S}$.
  • Glucose and titer come from samples taken by hand and analysed in the lab. Between samples, their entries are NaN, and the twin uses only what was measured at each time (see Models and sensors).
our(u, p) = p[7] * uptake(max(u[2], 0.0), p) * u[1]     # mmol O2 / L / h

σ = [0.8, 4.0, 0.02, 0.1, 0.05]
sensors = ObservationModel((u, p, t) -> [u[1], our(u, p), u[4], u[2], u[3]], σ;
    names = [:X_cap, :OUR, :V_load, :S_atline, :P_hplc]);

4. What can the data tell us?

Before choosing what to estimate, check what the sensors can see (see Identifiability). We change one parameter at a time and record the largest change in each measurement over the first 20 h, in units of that sensor's noise σ. Fewer than about 1σ is invisible. Many σ is easily learned.

function simulate(p; ts = 0.5:0.5:20.0)
    u, t, out = [0.2, 20.0, 0.0, 5.0], 0.0, Vector{Float64}[]
    for tk in ts
        u = Vardr.propagate(model, u, p, t, tk)
        t = tk
        push!(out, sensors.h(u, p, tk))
    end
    return reduce(hcat, out)
end
base = simulate(pd)
function visibility(name, factor)
    p = copy(pd)
    p[findfirst(==(name), PNAMES)] *= factor
    Δ = maximum(abs.(simulate(p) .- base); dims = 2) ./ σ
    return round.(vec(Δ); digits = 1)
end
changes = [(:mu_max, 0.9), (:Yxs, 0.9), (:qp, 0.9), (:f_pump, 0.95), (:ms, 2.0), (:Ks, 2.0)]
DataFrame([(; change = "$(name) × $(f)", (sensors.names .=> visibility(name, f))...)
           for (name, f) in changes])

How to read the table:

  • $\mu_{\max}$, $Y_{X/S}$ strongly affect OUR and glucose, so the data will pin them down.
  • $q_p$ is seen only by the HPLC, so it is learned only when a titer result arrives.
  • $f_\text{pump}$: a 5% error changes each 30-min reading by less than 2σ. But it shows up as a trend in the volume, so it accumulates over many readings, and only once the feed is large.
  • $K_s$ is almost invisible: glucose is either far above $K_s$ (batch phase) or near zero (fed batch). It is fixed at its literature value.
  • $m_S$ would be identifiable. It is fixed because it is well characterized for this strain, and estimating it alongside $Y_{X/S}$ would make the two compete for the same OUR signal.

So the twin estimates $\theta = (\mu_{\max}, Y_{X/S}, q_p, f_\text{pump})$.

5. A synthetic batch

To test the twin we need a "real" batch with a known answer (see Verifying a twin). This batch differs from the PD values in every estimated parameter. The cells grow 13% slower, yield 12% less biomass and produce 60% more protein. The pump delivers 8% less than requested, with ±3% fluctuations every 30 min. The inoculum is also 25% larger than assumed.

batch = copy(pd)
batch[[1, 3, 6, 13]] = [0.52, 0.44, 0.0065, 0.92]   # mu_max, Yxs, qp, f_pump

function run_plant(p, u, t0, t1; rng, dt = 0.5)
    traj = Dict{Float64, Vector{Float64}}()
    t = t0
    for tk in (t0 + dt):dt:t1
        pk = copy(p)
        pk[13] *= 1 + 0.03 * randn(rng)                # pump fluctuations
        u = Vardr.propagate(model, u, pk, t, tk)
        t = tk
        traj[tk] = u
    end
    return traj
end
rng = Xoshiro(3)
truth = run_plant(batch, [0.25, 20.0, 0.0, 5.0], 0.0, 20.0; rng)

data = map(0.5:0.5:20.0) do t
    u = truth[t]
    sampled = t % 4 == 0                             # lab samples every 4 h
    Observation(t, [u[1] + σ[1] * randn(rng), our(u, batch) + σ[2] * randn(rng),
        u[4] + σ[3] * randn(rng),
        sampled ? u[2] + σ[4] * randn(rng) : NaN,
        sampled ? u[3] + σ[5] * randn(rng) : NaN])
end

6. Configure the twin

Each setting has a reason:

  • Estimator: EnKF(N = 200). There are 8 unknowns (4 states and 4 parameters), the model is mildly nonlinear, and observations are frequent. A small inflation (1.02) offsets sampling error (see Bayesian filtering).
  • Prior: the PD values, with the batch-to-batch variation seen in development (about 15% on $\mu_{\max}$, 10% on $Y_{X/S}$, 50% on $q_p$, 5% on the pump).
  • Model error: small on states, to absorb the pump fluctuations and the simple kinetics. Very small on parameters, enough to keep their spread from collapsing (see Joint state–parameter estimation in practice).
  • Bounds: concentrations, volume and parameters are non-negative.
estimate = [:mu_max, :Yxs, :qp, :f_pump]
prior = MvNormal([0.2, 20.0, 0.0, 5.0, 0.6, 0.5, 0.004, 1.0],
    Diagonal([0.05, 0.5, 0.001, 0.05, 0.1, 0.05, 0.002, 0.05] .^ 2))
make_twin(estimator) = Twin(model, sensors; name = "batch-2417", estimator, estimate, prior,
    process_noise = [0.05, 0.005, 0.005, 0.002, 0.002, 0.002, 0.0001, 0.002],
    bounds = (zeros(8), fill(Inf, 8)), rng = Xoshiro(1))
twin = make_twin(EnKF(N = 200, inflation = 1.02))
Twin("batch-2417", t = 0.0, EnKF with 200 members)

The true values are $\mu_{\max} = 0.52$, $Y_{X/S} = 0.44$, $q_p = 0.0065$ and $f_\text{pump} = 0.92$. The order in which the twin learns follows the identifiability analysis:

  • During the batch phase (0 to 7 h), glucose is plentiful and cells grow at $\mu_{\max}$. The rising OUR and biomass reveal $\mu_{\max}$ and $Y_{X/S}$.
  • At 4 h and 8 h the first titers are still tiny, so $q_p$ stays at its prior. By 12 h two meaningful titers have arrived, and $q_p$ is pinned down.
  • The pump error cannot be seen until enough feed has been delivered for the volume trend to differ from the setpoint. At 12 h only about 0.15 L has been fed, and an 8% shortfall is about 0.01 L, below the load-cell noise. By 20 h the twin has found that the pump delivers 93 ± 2% of its setpoint.

This is joint state–parameter estimation at work (see Unknown parameters: the augmented state). No sensor measures $f_\text{pump}$ or $q_p$, but the ensemble learns how they correlate with what is measured.

8. Choose the feed cap

A chance constraint. The decision is an optimization under uncertainty:

\[\max_{F_\text{cap}}\ \mathbb E\big[P(t_h)V(t_h)\big] \quad\text{subject to}\quad \Pr\Big(\max_{t \le t_h}\mathrm{OUR}(t) > \mathrm{OTR}_{\max}\Big) \le 0.05 .\]

Requiring a constraint to hold with a given probability, rather than always, is called chance-constrained optimization (Charnes and Cooper, 1959). The twin's forecast estimates both quantities directly. For each candidate cap, run a scenario to harvest and evaluate the constraint on every sample (see What-if scenarios as interventions).

A detail matters here. OUR depends on the estimated parameters, so each forecast sample must be paired with its own parameters. With nsamples equal to the ensemble size (the default), sample $k$ of an EnKF forecast starts from member $k$. So members(twin)[:, k] gives sample $k$'s parameters.

const OTR_MAX, T_DECIDE, T_HARVEST = 180.0, 20.0, 36.0

function assess(tw, F_cap)
    fc = scenario(tw, SetParameter(T_DECIDE, :F_cap => F_cap), T_HARVEST;
        saveat = T_DECIDE:0.25:T_HARVEST)
    Z = members(tw)
    X, S, P, V = fc[:X], fc[:S], fc[:P], fc[:V]
    peak = map(axes(X, 2)) do k
        p = copy(pd)
        p[[1, 3, 6, 13]] = Z[5:8, k]                   # this sample's parameters
        maximum(our([X[j, k], S[j, k]], p) for j in axes(X, 1))
    end
    product = P[end, :] .* V[end, :]                   # grams of protein at harvest
    return (risk = mean(peak .> OTR_MAX), product = mean(product),
        lo = quantile(product, 0.05), hi = quantile(product, 0.95))
end

Under the recommended cap, the real batch stays below $\mathrm{OTR}_{\max} = 180$. Its product falls inside the twin's 90% interval, close to the forecast mean. A cap one step higher would have overloaded the vessel, as the twin predicted.

The twin also knows that the pump under-delivers. A recipe-based calculation that assumes $f_\text{pump} = 1$ would choose a more cautious cap and give up product for no reason. If the pump had over-delivered, the recipe would have overloaded the vessel.

10. In production

To run this twin on a real line:

  • Data in. Feed the online signals (capacitance, off-gas, load cell) through a ChannelSource from the plant historian or OPC UA server, with a WallClock so the twin keeps predicting through gaps (see Running live). Each lab result arrives as an Observation with NaN for the online channels.
  • Late lab results. HPLC results come back hours after sampling. The run loop assimilates observations in time order, and counts an observation that arrives after the twin has moved past it as late. To use the result, keep a snapshot at each sampling time, then restore! it and replay the buffered online data, including the lab value.
  • Alarms. Add a sink that forecasts the peak OUR every hour and raises an alarm when the overload probability exceeds a threshold. This is a predictive version of the usual dissolved-oxygen alarm.
  • Monitor the twin itself. Track the innovations of each sensor (see Without ground truth (real data): innovations). A sudden bias in one channel means either a sensor fault (such as a fouled capacitance probe) or a process change the model does not describe (such as the onset of acetate formation).
  • Learn across batches. Use each completed batch with calibrate! to update the PD values and the prior for the next batch.
  • Validation. In a GMP setting a model used for decisions is part of the control strategy. Its intended use, validation evidence (synthetic studies like this one, plus historical batches) and lifecycle management must be documented (International Council for Harmonisation, 2009).
