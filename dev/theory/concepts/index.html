Digital twins and state-space models

This chapter explains what Vardr computes and why. The next chapters cover Bayesian filtering (how the twin learns from data) and forecasting, calibration and diagnostics. Each section ends with a short In Vardr note that maps the theory onto the API.

What is a digital twin?

The term goes back to product lifecycle management (Grieves and Vickers, 2017). A recent consensus report by the US National Academies defines a digital twin as

a set of virtual information constructs that mimics the structure, context, and behavior of a natural, engineered, or social system (or system-of-systems), is dynamically updated with data from its physical twin, has a predictive capability, and informs decisions that realize value. The bidirectional interaction between the virtual and the physical is central to the digital twin. (National Academies of Sciences, Engineering, and Medicine, 2024)

Three ingredients separate a twin from an ordinary simulation:

  1. A model of one specific asset or individual: this pump, this patient, this building. It is not a generic model of pumps.
  2. Continuous updating from data. As measurements arrive, the twin's estimate of the asset's current state, and of its uncertain properties, is corrected. In the geosciences this is called data assimilation (Evensen, 2007; Asch et al., 2016).
  3. Prediction in support of decisions. The updated twin forecasts what will happen, and what would happen under alternative actions, with quantified uncertainty (Kapteyn et al., 2021).

The loop that connects them:

          ┌──────────────── physical system ◀──────────── decisions ─────┐
          │                                                              │
     measurements y_k                                                    │
          ▼                                                              │
  ┌─ assimilate ─┐     ┌─ predict ─┐                ┌─ forecast / what-if ┐
  │ p(z_k|y_1:k) │ ──▶ │ p(z_k+1|  │ ──▶  ...  ──▶  │ p(z_t+τ | y_1:k,    │
  └──────────────┘     │   y_1:k)  │                │   intervention)     │
                       └───────────┘                └─────────────────────┘

Vardr implements the inner part of this loop: assimilation, prediction, forecasting and what-if analysis. The decision itself is yours.

The state-space model

Vardr describes a system in state-space form, the standard formulation of filtering theory (Jazwinski, 1970; Särkkä, 2013).

Process (dynamics). The state $u(t) \in \mathbb{R}^{n}$ evolves according to an ordinary differential equation with parameters $p$:

\[\frac{\mathrm{d}u}{\mathrm{d}t} = f(u, p, t).\]

Between two observation times $t_{k-1}$ and $t_k$, we write the solution map (the flow) as

\[u_k = \mathcal{M}_{t_{k-1} \to t_k}(u_{k-1}, p).\]

The model is never perfect. Unmodelled physics, unknown inputs and discretization errors are lumped into a model error $\eta_k$:

\[u_k = \mathcal{M}_{t_{k-1} \to t_k}(u_{k-1}, p) + \eta_k .\]

Observation. At time $t_k$, sensors measure

\[y_k = h(u_k, p, t_k) + \varepsilon_k, \qquad \varepsilon_k \sim \mathcal{N}(0, R),\]

where $h$ is the observation operator and $R$ the measurement-error covariance. Usually $h$ returns a few components of $u$ (temperature at a sensor, the level of a tank). It can also be any nonlinear function, such as a drug effect computed from a concentration.

Prior. Before any data, our knowledge of the state is a probability distribution $p(u_0)$.

In Vardr
  • $f$, the nominal $u_0$ and $p$: TwinModel. The flow $\mathcal{M}$ is computed by a SciML ODE solver (Rackauckas and Nie, 2017), by default Tsit5 (Tsitouras, 2011). ModelingToolkit models (Ma et al., 2021) work too.
  • $h$ and $R$: ObservationModel. ObservationModel(model, [:x], σ) means $h(u) = u_x$ and $R = \operatorname{diag}(\sigma^2)$.
  • $y_k$ at $t_k$: Observation(t_k, y_k).
  • $p(u_0)$: the prior keyword of Twin.
  • $\eta_k$: the process_noise keyword (see Model error below).

Unknown parameters: the augmented state

Some parameters $\theta \subset p$ are not known well enough: a heat-transfer coefficient, a patient's drug clearance, a leak. Treating them as random variables, and appending them to the state, turns parameter estimation into state estimation (Kitagawa, 1998; Evensen, 2009):

\[z = \begin{bmatrix} u \\ \theta \end{bmatrix}, \qquad z_k = \begin{bmatrix} \mathcal{M}(u_{k-1}, \theta_{k-1}) \\ \theta_{k-1} \end{bmatrix} + \eta_k .\]

Parameters have no dynamics of their own: they are constant apart from the noise $\eta_k$. Data still informs them, because the filter learns the correlation between $\theta$ and the observed outputs. If ensemble members with a larger leak coefficient predict a lower tank level, and the measured level is low, the update shifts the leak coefficient upwards. This is called joint (or augmented) state–parameter estimation (Moradkhani et al., 2005).

In Vardr

Twin(model, sensors; estimate = [:k, :c]) builds $z = [u; k; c]$. Everything Vardr computes (belief, history, forecasts) is over $z$. Parameters that are not in estimate stay fixed at their values in the model.

Model error

Vardr models $\eta$ as a random walk with independent components:

\[\eta_k \sim \mathcal{N}\!\big(0,\ \Delta t_k \operatorname{diag}(q)^2\big), \qquad \Delta t_k = t_k - t_{k-1},\]

where $q_i$ is the standard deviation per unit time of component $i$. The variance grows linearly with the time between observations, as for a Wiener process, so one setting works for irregular sampling.

Model error plays two roles:

  • On states, it represents real unpredictability: disturbances and missing physics. Without it, the twin trusts its model too much and stops following the data, which is called filter divergence (Jazwinski, 1970).
  • On parameters, it is an artificial evolution that lets the parameter ensemble keep a nonzero spread, and lets estimates follow slow real changes such as wear or fouling (Kitagawa, 1998; Liu and West, 2001).
In Vardr

process_noise = q (a scalar, or one value per component of $z$). It is applied after every predict!. Members are then clipped to bounds, a simple way to enforce physical constraints such as non-negative concentrations.

Uncertainty is represented by samples

The quantity a twin maintains is the filtering distribution $p(z_k \mid y_{1:k})$: everything we know about the system now, given all data so far. For nonlinear models this distribution has no closed form. Vardr represents it by $N$ samples (members) $z^{(1)}, \dots, z^{(N)}$, optionally with weights:

\[p(z_k \mid y_{1:k}) \approx \sum_{i=1}^{N} w^{(i)} \, \delta\big(z - z_k^{(i)}\big).\]

Every summary is a sample statistic: the mean $\bar z = \sum_i w^{(i)} z^{(i)}$, the covariance, quantiles, and probabilities of events. This Monte Carlo representation is shared by the ensemble Kalman filter (equal weights $w^{(i)} = 1/N$) and the particle filter (unequal weights), described in the next chapter.

In Vardr

The belief is EnsembleBelief or ParticleBelief. twin[:x] is the mean, uncertainty the standard deviation, and members the samples themselves.

Two kinds of uncertainty

It is useful to separate:

  • Epistemic uncertainty: lack of knowledge about the state and parameters. Data reduces it: the posterior spread of $\theta$ shrinks as the twin learns.
  • Aleatoric uncertainty: inherent randomness, such as sensor noise $\varepsilon$ and disturbances $\eta$. Data does not reduce it.

A good twin reports both honestly. Overconfidence (too little spread) is the most common failure in practice, and the most dangerous for decisions. Diagnostics that detect it are covered in Verifying a twin.
