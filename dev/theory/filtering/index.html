Bayesian filtering and data assimilation

This chapter derives the algorithms that keep a twin synchronized with data. The notation follows Digital twins and state-space models: the augmented state is $z = [u; \theta]$ and the observations are $y_k = h(z_k) + \varepsilon_k$ with $\varepsilon_k \sim \mathcal N(0, R)$.

The filtering recursion

The goal is the filtering distribution $p(z_k \mid y_{1:k})$. Because the model is Markov (the next state depends only on the current one) and each observation depends only on the current state, it can be computed recursively, one observation at a time (Jazwinski, 1970; Särkkä, 2013).

Prediction (Chapman–Kolmogorov equation): push the current belief through the dynamics.

\[p(z_k \mid y_{1:k-1}) = \int p(z_k \mid z_{k-1}) \, p(z_{k-1} \mid y_{1:k-1}) \, \mathrm{d}z_{k-1}\]

Update (Bayes' rule): weigh the prediction by how well it explains the new measurement.

\[p(z_k \mid y_{1:k}) = \frac{p(y_k \mid z_k)\, p(z_k \mid y_{1:k-1})}{p(y_k \mid y_{1:k-1})}, \qquad p(y_k \mid z_k) = \mathcal N\big(y_k;\ h(z_k),\ R\big).\]

The prediction is also called the forecast or prior, and the update the analysis. Prediction spreads uncertainty, because of chaos and model error; the update contracts it.

In Vardr

predict!(twin, t) is the prediction step and assimilate!(twin, obs) the update step. step! does both. All estimators share the prediction step: every member is integrated with the ODE solver, in parallel over threads, and then model error is added:

\[z_k^{(i)} = \begin{bmatrix}\mathcal M(u_{k-1}^{(i)}, \theta_{k-1}^{(i)}) \\ \theta_{k-1}^{(i)}\end{bmatrix} + \sqrt{\Delta t_k}\; q \odot \xi^{(i)}, \qquad \xi^{(i)} \sim \mathcal N(0, I).\]

The estimators differ only in the update.

The Kalman filter

If the dynamics and $h$ are linear and all distributions are Gaussian, the recursion has an exact closed form, the Kalman filter (Kalman, 1960). With the predicted mean $z^f$ and covariance $P^f$, and $h(z) = Hz$, the update is

\[\begin{aligned} K &= P^f H^\top \big(H P^f H^\top + R\big)^{-1} &&\text{(Kalman gain)}\\ z^a &= z^f + K\,\big(y - H z^f\big) &&\text{(analysis mean)}\\ P^a &= (I - K H)\, P^f &&\text{(analysis covariance)} \end{aligned}\]

The gain $K$ weighs model against data. A confident model (small $P^f$) moves little; a confident sensor (small $R$) pulls the estimate towards the measurement. Note that $P^f H^\top$ is the covariance between the state and the predicted observation, and $HP^fH^\top$ is the covariance of the predicted observation:

\[K = \operatorname{Cov}(z, h(z))\, \big[\operatorname{Cov}(h(z)) + R\big]^{-1}.\]

This form needs no matrix $H$ and no explicit $P^f$, only covariances, which can be estimated from samples. That is the key idea of the ensemble Kalman filter.

The ensemble Kalman filter (EnKF)

The EnKF (Evensen, 1994; Evensen, 2007) represents the predicted distribution by $N$ equally weighted members $z^{(i)}$. It estimates the covariances in the Kalman gain from the ensemble, and applies the Kalman update to every member. The model can be nonlinear, because each member is propagated through the full nonlinear model. The Gaussian assumption is made only in the update.

The algorithm Vardr implements

Given the predicted members $Z = [z^{(1)}, \dots, z^{(N)}]$ and an observation $y$ with covariance $R$:

  1. Inflation (optional, factor $\lambda \ge 1$): $z^{(i)} \leftarrow \bar z + \lambda\,(z^{(i)} - \bar z)$.
  2. Predicted observations: $\hat y^{(i)} = h(u^{(i)}, \theta^{(i)}, t_k)$. Missing entries (NaN) of $y$ are removed, together with the matching rows of $\hat y$ and rows and columns of $R$.
  3. Anomalies and sample covariances:

    \[A = Z - \bar z\,\mathbf 1^\top, \quad B = \hat Y - \bar{\hat y}\,\mathbf 1^\top, \quad C_{zy} = \frac{A B^\top}{N-1}, \quad C_{yy} = \frac{B B^\top}{N-1}.\]

  4. Perturbed observations (Burgers et al., 1998; Houtekamer and Mitchell, 1998): each member sees its own noisy copy of the measurement, $y^{(i)} = y + \varepsilon^{(i)}$, with $\varepsilon^{(i)} \sim \mathcal N(0, R)$.
  5. Update every member:

    \[z^{(i)} \leftarrow z^{(i)} + C_{zy}\,\big(C_{yy} + R\big)^{-1}\big(y^{(i)} - \hat y^{(i)}\big).\]

Why perturb the observations? Without step 4, every member would move by the same deterministic amount, and the analysis spread would be too small. With perturbed observations, the analysis ensemble has the correct Kalman covariance $(I - KH)P^f$ in expectation (Burgers et al., 1998).

Inflation

With finite $N$, sampling errors make the EnKF systematically underestimate its own uncertainty. The spread shrinks a little too much at every update until the filter ignores the data. Multiplicative inflation (Anderson and Anderson, 1999) counteracts this by spreading the members about their mean before each update. Typical values are 1.01–1.10. Model error (process_noise) acts similarly, but it is additive and applied during prediction.

Localization

With fewer members than unknowns, sample covariances contain spurious long-range correlations. In a 40-cell heat model with 10 members, a sensor at one end appears correlated with the other end purely by chance, and the update corrupts distant cells. Covariance localization (Houtekamer and Mitchell, 2001; Hamill et al., 2001) multiplies covariances elementwise (a Schur product, $\circ$) by a taper that decays with distance:

\[K_u = \big(L_{uy} \circ C_{uy}\big)\big(L_{yy} \circ C_{yy} + R\big)^{-1}.\]

Here $L_{uy}$ tapers state–observation pairs and $L_{yy}$ observation–observation pairs. A common taper is the compactly supported, fifth-order piecewise rational function of (Gaspari and Cohn, 1999), which looks like a Gaussian but is exactly zero beyond twice its length scale.

Both tapers must come from the same correlation function. The gain is then the exact Kalman gain of the localized covariance $L \circ P^f$, which is positive semi-definite by the Schur product theorem. Tapering only $C_{uy}$ is a common shortcut, but it can increase the spread at the sensors and make the filter diverge.

Estimated parameters $\theta$ are global (they affect every location), so they have no meaningful "distance". Vardr updates them with the unlocalized gain $C_{\theta y}(C_{yy} + R)^{-1}$.

In Vardr

EnKF(N, inflation = λ, localization = (Lxy, Lyy)). If the observation model selects states directly, Lyy is the rows of Lxy at the observed states, so you can pass Lxy alone. See examples/heat_rod_twin.jl for a Gaspari–Cohn implementation. There, localization cuts the error by a factor of three with only 10 members.

Strengths and limits of the EnKF

  • Cost: $N$ model runs per step, independent of the state dimension. It scales to very large models, especially with localization.
  • Robustness: members are moved, not reweighted, so the EnKF does not degenerate.
  • Gaussian update: the update is linear in the innovation. For strongly nonlinear $h$, or for bimodal or skewed posteriors, the EnKF is approximate. It is still usually accurate when observations are frequent compared with the system's nonlinear time scales.
  • Bounds: the linear update can push members across physical limits. Vardr clips them to bounds afterwards.

The particle filter

The bootstrap particle filter (Gordon et al., 1993) makes no Gaussian assumption. It represents the belief by weighted particles and applies Bayes' rule literally.

  1. Predict each particle through the model (as above).
  2. Weight each particle by the likelihood of the observation:

    \[\log w^{(i)} \leftarrow \log w^{(i)} + \log \mathcal N\big(y;\ h(z^{(i)}),\ R\big).\]

  3. Resample when the weights become uneven. The effective sample size

    \[\mathrm{ESS} = \frac{1}{\sum_i \tilde w_i^2}, \qquad \tilde w_i = \frac{w_i}{\sum_j w_j}\]

    is $N$ for equal weights and 1 when a single particle holds all the weight (Doucet and Johansen, 2011). If $\mathrm{ESS} < \tau N$, Vardr draws $N$ new particles with systematic resampling (Kitagawa, 1996), which uses one random number and has low variance, and resets the weights to equal.
  4. Jitter (optional): after resampling, duplicated particles are identical. Adding noise $\sigma \odot j\,\xi$, where $\sigma$ is the posterior standard deviation, restores diversity. This matters most for parameters, which have no dynamics of their own to spread them out (Liu and West, 2001).

The particle filter converges to the exact Bayesian solution as $N \to \infty$, for any nonlinearity. The price is the curse of dimensionality: the number of particles needed grows exponentially with the effective dimension of the problem (Snyder et al., 2008). Use it for small problems (≲ 10 unknowns) with strong nonlinearity or multimodality, and the EnKF otherwise.

In Vardr

ParticleFilter(N, resample_threshold = τ, jitter = j). If every particle has zero likelihood (all particle weights are zero), the observation is incompatible with the whole belief. Widen the prior, add process noise, or check R.

Joint state–parameter estimation in practice

With the augmented state, the update of a parameter is driven by the sample correlation between that parameter and the predicted observations. In EnKF terms:

\[\Delta\theta^{(i)} = C_{\theta y}\,(C_{yy} + R)^{-1}\,\big(y^{(i)} - \hat y^{(i)}\big).\]

Practical consequences (Evensen, 2009; Moradkhani et al., 2005):

  • Identifiability. If changing $\theta$ barely changes the measured outputs, $C_{\theta y} \approx 0$ and the parameter is never updated. Its uncertainty stays at the prior. This is a property of the model and sensors, not of the filter. See Identifiability.
  • Delayed sensitivity. Some parameters only affect outputs after some time, or under certain conditions (excitation). A heater efficiency is invisible while the heater is off. Learning then happens in bursts.
  • Collapse. Repeated updates shrink the parameter spread. Without artificial evolution (process_noise on $\theta$) the spread eventually reaches zero, and the twin can no longer track changes.
  • Abrupt changes (a valve fails, a leak opens) need larger parameter noise, or a detection rule on the innovations. See examples/tank_leak_detection_twin.jl.
