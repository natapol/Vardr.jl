Prediction, calibration and verification

Forecasting: the predictive distribution

A forecast answers "what will the system do over the next $\tau$ time units, given everything observed so far?" Formally, it is the predictive distribution

\[p(u_{t+\tau} \mid y_{1:k}) = \int p(u_{t+\tau} \mid z_k)\, p(z_k \mid y_{1:k})\, \mathrm{d}z_k .\]

Vardr evaluates it by Monte Carlo: draw samples $z^{(s)}$ from the current belief and integrate each one forward with its own parameters:

\[u_{t+\tau}^{(s)} = \mathcal M_{t \to t+\tau}\big(u^{(s)}, \theta^{(s)}\big), \qquad s = 1, \dots, S.\]

The forecast spread therefore combines state uncertainty and parameter uncertainty, and it grows with the horizon. Chaotic systems grow it exponentially, at a rate set by the largest Lyapunov exponent (Lorenz, 1963). The horizon over which a twin forecast is useful is therefore limited, and the ensemble spread is an estimate of that limit (see examples/lorenz63_twin.jl).

Vardr's forecasts are deterministic given the sample: model error $\eta$ is not added. They represent uncertainty about the present system, not about future random disturbances. If future disturbances matter, for example weather, include them as explicit scenarios or as uncertain inputs.

In Vardr

forecast(twin, t_end; nsamples = S, saveat). For an EnKF with S = N, each member is used exactly once; otherwise samples are drawn according to the belief's weights. Any probability is a sample average, for example $P(\max_t x > c) \approx \frac1S \sum_s \mathbb 1[\max_t x^{(s)}(t) > c]$, which is mean(maximum(fc[:x]; dims = 1) .> c) in code.

What-if scenarios as interventions

A what-if question asks for the predictive distribution under an action $a$: a change of parameter, state or input at a time $t_a$. Vardr replaces the affected quantity in the model at $t_a$ and integrates on, which is an intervention on the mechanistic model:

\[p(u_{t+\tau} \mid y_{1:k}, \mathrm{do}(a)).\]

Because each option is evaluated on the same samples of the current belief, differences between scenarios reflect the actions, not sampling noise. This is the method of common random numbers. Decisions should then be based on decision-relevant summaries: the expected value of a cost, the probability of violating a limit, or a quantile. Do not use the mean trajectory alone: the mean of a nonlinear function is not the function of the mean.

In Vardr

scenario(twin, interventions, t_end) with SetParameter, SetState and Event. Internally these are SciML DiscreteCallbacks at tstops, so the solver stops exactly at $t_a$.

Calibration: maximum likelihood

Online estimation learns parameters sequentially. Calibration fits them to a batch of historical data at once. Under the observation model, and treating the model as exact over the calibration window, the likelihood of the data is

\[\mathcal L(\theta) = \prod_k \mathcal N\big(y_k;\ h(u_k(\theta), \theta),\ R\big), \qquad u_k(\theta) = \mathcal M_{t_0 \to t_k}(u_0, \theta),\]

and the maximum-likelihood estimate minimizes the negative log-likelihood (dropping constants):

\[\hat\theta = \arg\min_\theta\ \frac12 \sum_k r_k(\theta)^\top R^{-1} r_k(\theta), \qquad r_k(\theta) = y_k - h\big(u_k(\theta), \theta\big).\]

With diagonal $R$ this is weighted least squares, each residual being scaled by its sensor's noise. Missing values are left out of $r_k$.

Calibration and filtering complement each other:

Usesall data at once, a deterministic modeldata sequentially, model error allowed
Givesa point estimate $\hat\theta$a distribution that evolves over time

Typical usecommissioning, from historical dataoperation

For a full Bayesian treatment of calibration, including model discrepancy, see (Kennedy and O'Hagan, 2001).

In Vardr

calibrate!(twin, data, [:k, :c]; alg) minimizes exactly the objective above with any Optimization.jl optimizer. It then writes $\hat\theta$ into the twin's fixed parameters, or recentres the members of parameters that are also estimated online.

Identifiability

A parameter is identifiable if the data can determine it. A parameter is structurally non-identifiable if different values produce identical outputs: for example, only a product $ab$ affects the measurement. It is practically non-identifiable if the output differences are smaller than the noise, or the data does not excite the relevant dynamics (Raue et al., 2009).

A quick practical check before building a twin: simulate the outputs with each parameter perturbed by its prior uncertainty, and compare the change in $h$ with the sensor noise $\sigma$. Changes much smaller than $\sigma$, over the time scales of interest, mean the data will not inform that parameter. Fix the parameter, measure something else, or accept that its uncertainty will stay at the prior. The Step-by-step workflow shows this check in code.

Verifying a twin: is the uncertainty honest?

A twin is useful for decisions only if its uncertainty is calibrated: when it says "90% interval", the truth should fall inside about 90% of the time. Over-confidence is dangerous; under-confidence wastes information. The tools below come from ensemble forecast verification (Hamill, 2001; Gneiting and Raftery, 2007) and from data-assimilation diagnostics (Desroziers et al., 2005).

With ground truth (synthetic experiments)

Before using real data, run an observing-system simulation experiment (OSSE, also called a twin experiment). Simulate a "true" system with known parameters, generate noisy observations from it, and check that the twin recovers the truth. Every Vardr example is such an experiment. Check:

  • Accuracy: the root-mean-square error of the posterior mean is well below that of an open-loop simulation (NoAssimilation).
  • Coverage: the fraction of times the truth lies within $\bar z \pm m\sigma$. For a Gaussian, this is ≈ 68% for $m = 1$ and ≈ 95% for $m = 2$.
  • Rank histogram: the rank of the truth among the sorted members should be uniform (Hamill, 2001). A U shape means too little spread; a dome shape means too much.

Without ground truth (real data): innovations

With real data the truth is unknown, but the measurement is not. The innovation is the difference between a measurement and the twin's prediction of it, before the update:

\[d_k = y_k - \overline{h(z_k^f)}, \qquad S_k = \operatorname{Cov}\big(h(z_k^f)\big) + R .\]

If the twin is consistent, $d_k$ has mean zero and covariance $S_k$ (Desroziers et al., 2005). Two practical statistics:

  • Bias: the mean of $d_k$ over time should be ≈ 0. A persistent bias indicates a model error that the twin cannot explain: missing physics, a drifting sensor, or a parameter outside the prior.
  • Normalized innovation squared: $\mathrm{NIS}_k = d_k^\top S_k^{-1} d_k$ should average to the number of observed outputs (1 for a single sensor). If it is much larger, the twin is over-confident: increase process noise or inflation. If it is much smaller, the twin is under-confident, or $R$ is too large.

Monitoring innovations online is also a powerful anomaly detector. A sudden run of large innovations means the real system has stopped behaving like its twin.

In Vardr

Every prediction (kind = :predict) and update (kind = :update) is recorded in history. For sensors that observe states directly, the predicted mean and standard deviation of the observed state are in the :predict rows, which is enough to compute $d_k$ and the NIS. The Step-by-step workflow does this.

Sequential processing of live data

Filtering is sequential: observations must be assimilated in time order. In live operation, data can arrive late, out of order, or not at all. Vardr's runner uses these rules:

  • An observation newer than the twin is assimilated normally.
  • During silence, an optional wall clock advances the twin by prediction only, so the uncertainty grows honestly. These advances are provisional. If a delayed observation then arrives, the twin rolls back to the last checkpoint and assimilates it. This is the same as having waited for it.
  • An observation older than the last assimilated one cannot be used without re-running the filter from an earlier state. It is counted as late and dropped.
In Vardr

run!, WallClock, and the late, rollbacks and failed counters on TwinRunner.
